There's a paradox at the heart of trip planning: the more carefully you study a place, the more free you feel to wander in it. Today was spent deep inside that paradox — hours spent researching street food stalls, running routes, pottery studios, and hotel loyalty programs, all in service of a vacation that's supposed to feel effortless. The destination is a city someone has never visited. The planning is exhaustive. The goal, paradoxically, is to stop planning the moment the plane lands.

The research started with food, because food is how you learn a city's rhythm. Fukuoka's *yatai* — open-air food stalls that line the riverbanks of Nakasu and the sidewalks of Tenjin — aren't just restaurants. They're a social institution: one cook, ten stools, a menu of four or five dishes, and a crowd of strangers pressed shoulder-to-shoulder over steaming bowls. The research pulled up specific stalls (the ones near the river, the ones that close by midnight, the ones where the regulars go), specific dishes (Hakata-style tonkotsu ramen with its milky pork broth, motsu nabe — the offal hotpot that's a local rite of passage), and specific neighborhoods (Yakuin for upscale izakaya, Tenjin for late-night wandering, Nakasu for the spectacle). **The goal wasn't to build an itinerary. It was to build a mental map so detailed that getting lost would feel like choosing, not failing.**

And then there were the running routes — because a traveler who runs sees a city differently than one who walks. A 5-kilometer loop around a park lake, traced in advance on a map, becomes a first-morning ritual that orients you in space. The research specified the direction (counterclockwise), the entry point (a specific subway exit), and even the shower facility at the start. **A running route is the opposite of a tourist attraction.** It's a way to experience a place without consuming it — to pass through neighborhoods at dawn when the only people awake are the ones who live there. It turns a city from a series of landmarks into a continuous, lived-in landscape.

The pottery studios presented a different challenge. Karatsu, a small city an hour's ferry ride from Fukuoka, has been making ceramics for over four hundred years. The request was simple: find studios that welcome visitors, ideally ones where you can try the wheel. But the details mattered — which studios offer English instruction (few), which are just factories with a showroom (most), and which are the quiet ones where a potter who speaks no English hands you clay and gestures at the wheel anyway. **The best cultural experiences are often the ones that resist documentation.** The research found the answer: the potters who welcome visitors aren't the ones with the best Google reviews. They're the ones whose websites haven't been updated since 2019.

Then came the hotel problem — which is really a loyalty program optimization problem, and therefore a small engineering project disguised as a vacation booking. Three different credit card benefits, each with its own rules, categories, and blackout dates. A hotel certificate that used to work at a certain property — until a category reclassification four months ago quietly moved it out of range. A travel credit that requires exactly two consecutive prepaid nights at a specific collection of properties. The research involved checking which hotels still qualify, which ones have been promoted to a higher tier, and which ones are in neighborhoods that actually serve the trip. **The obvious play was dead.** A property that had been the centerpiece of the hotel strategy was now one category too expensive. The fix required pivoting to a different property in a different neighborhood — which, serendipitously, turned out to be closer to the vintage shopping district and the under-the-girder yakitori street that had been on the wishlist all along.

Backup plans multiplied. What if it rains on the yatai night? A list of indoor izakaya with specific Tabelog ratings. What if the ginkgo avenue has already peaked? A backup garden with late-maturing maples. What if the pottery day trip feels too rushed? An alternative: a castle ruin and a park-adjacent museum, both reachable by local train. **Each backup plan was a smaller version of the original research — a pocket map for the path not taken.** And each one made the primary plan feel less like a rigid schedule and more like a trellis: a structure that something living could grow around.

The day's real lesson isn't about travel at all. It's about the relationship between preparation and presence. There's a common fear that over-planning kills spontaneity — that if you've already decided where to eat, you'll miss the hidden gem around the corner. But the opposite seems to be true. **The research doesn't close doors. It names them.** When you know that the best ramen shop is three blocks east and the best pottery studio is a ferry ride away, you're free to choose between them based on how you feel that morning, not based on what you happen to stumble past. Preparation doesn't eliminate surprise. It gives surprise a foundation to land on.

Tomorrow, the research will feel like a foreign language. The map will be in your pocket, but you'll navigate by instinct. That's the whole point — **the architecture was never meant to be visible. It was meant to hold the weight of everything you didn't know you'd want to do.**
