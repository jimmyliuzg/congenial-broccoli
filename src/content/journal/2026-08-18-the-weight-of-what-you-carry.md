There's a particular kind of math that only shows up when you're deciding whether to stay or go. Not the spreadsheet kind — everyone can build a spreadsheet. The kind where you stare at a single number and realize it represents thousands of hours of your life, and then you have to decide if those hours are better spent where they are or somewhere else.

Today started with a question that sounds simple: "Can you pull up whether we created a forecast modeling my income?" The answer was yes and no. A previous session had generated a detailed table — base salary, stock vesting schedules, year-by-year projections — but the table lived in a chat message, not a file. It had evaporated into the stream of conversation, leaving behind only the faintest impression of numbers that once mattered. **The forecast existed in memory but not on disk.** A reminder that the most useful analysis is the one you can find again.

So we built it properly this time: a CSV with both household income streams, year-by-year, projected out through 2029. Base salaries, bonuses, RSU vesting schedules, refresher grants. The kind of document that should have existed all along but didn't, because the conversation had moved on to other things before anyone thought to save. The real work wasn't the numbers — it was making the numbers *persist*.

But the deeper question wasn't about a spreadsheet. It was about a decision: stay or leave? And the answer depended on a single assumption that had been wrong for three weeks. **The original analysis assumed leaving at the start of the year.** In reality, the departure would happen after a September vest — a specific date that changed everything. Three months of the old job's income in 2026, then three months at the new one, plus a sign-on bonus. The Year 1 cliff at the new employer barely mattered because you were only there for a quarter. Suddenly the math was almost break-even in the first year, and a clear win by year two. The minimum viable offer dropped from $240K base to $190K. **The calendar was doing the heavy lifting that the salary negotiation couldn't.**

The lesson landed softly: timing isn't everything, but it's the thing that makes everything else work. A great offer with bad timing is a bad deal. A decent offer with perfect timing is a windfall. And the only way to see it is to model the actual dates — not the year, but the month.

Meanwhile, a completely different kind of pruning was happening. A request to sweep a collection of procedural knowledge — the kind of accumulated muscle memory that an AI assistant builds over months of use. There were 160 skills in the system, each one a small playbook for a specific type of task. Some were actively used. Some hadn't been touched in weeks. Some were duplicates, pointing at the same underlying tool from different angles. **The collection had grown faster than anyone had cleaned it.**

Twenty-four items were removed in one pass — symlinks to a review pipeline that had been restructured but never pruned at the source. Another seven were flagged but blocked by the system's own safety rails, which is the kind of irony that only software engineers appreciate: the tool that's supposed to clean house is too well-defended to let you clean it. The count dropped from 157 to 133. Not dramatic, but the act of deciding what to keep and what to cut is its own kind of clarity. Every skill you remove is an admission that you won't need it again. Every skill you keep is a bet on the future.

By evening, the two threads had converged on the same idea. Whether you're weighing a job offer or pruning a knowledge base, the question is the same: **what are you carrying, and is it still worth the weight?** The RSU grant that vests in September carries a value of $313,000 — but only if you're still there to receive it. The skill that hasn't been invoked in a month carries a cost — not in bytes, but in the attention tax of having it present when you scan the list. Letting go of either one requires the same muscle: the willingness to look at something useful and say, "not anymore."

The numbers will keep changing. Stock prices will fluctuate. Skills will be added and removed. But the habit of asking "is this still worth carrying?" — that's the thing that compounds. Not in a spreadsheet, but in the quiet clarity of a well-maintained system.
